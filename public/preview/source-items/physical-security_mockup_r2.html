Tamper-evident seals, enclosures and cameras for AI hardware

The idea

Inspectors checking international AI agreements need to see whether AI chips, servers, racks and power-measuring devices held by another party have been physically opened or altered. Proposed means include tamper-evident seals, locks, screws painted with glitter nail polish and photographed, 24/7 video cameras, and enclosures built around a physical unclonable function, several adapted from nuclear materials monitoring and retrofitted to existing data centres within one to three years.

Why it matters

Physical attacks require handling the hardware, which makes them easier to detect, and retrofitted tamper evidence could make claims based on verification hardware more credible. Yet public evidence on high-end seals is limited, one study cheaply defeated all 289 seals it assessed, and full anti-tamper assurance against a highly capable adversary is unlikely to be achieved soon, if ever.

Possible interpretation (ours, not the sources')

Details: The aim of this project is to provide a minimal level of tamper evidence that could be retrofitted to existing data centers to enable credible guarantees. This may include strategies to minimize maintenance requirements or structured access protocols to prevent maintenance operations from being used to disguise tampering. Ideally, the complete proposed solution would undergo external red-team security assessment and usability testing.

Building AI Security & Verification Infrastructure Before We Need It

In the late 1950s, negotiations for a comprehensive nuclear test ban began. Until the late 70s, a full ban was blocked because neither the U.S. nor the USSR could reliably detect small underground tests. Without credible verification, both sides feared the other could cheat undetected. Only in the 90s was the political climate and verification technology mature enough for a full test ban. That technical gap led to decades of extra nuclear testing. Each new test deepened mistrust, justified larger arsenals, and made future agreements politically radioactive. The lack of verification technology didn't just delay one treaty; it entrenched a destabilising arms race that might have been avoided.

We may be heading toward a similar inflection point with AI—but the challenge is broader than verification alone.

On the security side: As AI systems become capable of accelerating AI R&D itself, the value concentrated in model weights, training secrets, and agentic scaffolds becomes enormous. Nation-states and other well-resourced actors will have strong incentives to steal this IP. We'll need security measures that don't yet exist at the required level of robustness: tamper-evident infrastructure, hardware-enforced constraints on data movement, confidential computing environments, and systems that can monitor and constrain even sophisticated AI models attempting to exfiltrate themselves.

On the verification side: International agreements between AI superpowers will require trust in each other's development and deployment practices. This means being able to verify compute usage, workload types, and compliance with agreements—none of which is possible with today's infrastructure.

The problem is lead time. Security and verification systems take years to design, build, deploy, battle-test, and integrate into real infrastructure. The tools we'll need for powerful AI systems must be built and deployed before those systems arrive. Research papers and theoretical frameworks aren't enough—we need working systems that have been hardened through actual use. If we wait until the need is acute, we'll be years behind—and those years could see unchecked proliferation, destabilizing theft of AI capabilities, or coordination failures that might otherwise have been avoided.

There is a unique opportunity for founders and engineers: build and ship real security and verification infrastructure now—before the world urgently needs it—to enable both robust protection of AI systems and credible international coordination on the most important technology of our time.

## The Idea

A Physical Unclonable Function (PUF) integrated into a tamper-evident enclosure, such that any physical intrusion changes the PUF output. The PUF output is used as a cryptographic key for decrypting firmware, authenticating the device, or protecting sensitive data. Sealing the information perimeter around monitored compute

The idea

Several proposed arrangements for checking what workloads run on clusters of AI chips place the hardware inside a closed physical perimeter: an air gap, Faraday cages, tamper-evident taps on every network link and controlled personnel exits, so that no data or component leaves except through monitored channels. The perimeter must also stop covert electromagnetic, power-line, acoustic and timing leaks, holding covert bandwidth below kilobits per second rather than zero, with enclosures, filters and jamming that can be mass-produced and retrofitted.

Why it matters

The arrangements rely on intercepting communication rather than securing the computation on the chip, and an operator who smuggles data past the taps through physical side channels could run undeclared workloads and exfiltrate the results. TEMPEST-grade SCIFs are judged inadequate for mass deployment, and TEMPEST-grade power filters are low-volume and mostly hand-assembled.

Prior work

Side-channel research is a science of its own, and has been long before the recent surge of interest in AI. In contrast to many of the open research questions listed so far, this one is far less neglected, at least when it comes to the discovery of exploits. But when it comes to defense, and the specifics of our threat model (covert adversary attempting to run undeclared ML workloads on monitored hardware and exfiltrate results), related work has overlapping, but different goals.

Side-channel defenses need to be just as retrofittable, cost efficient and scalable as the rest of the reference architecture. This problem motivated my recent survey on side-channels, and possible mitigations. While details are to be found in that report, I want to highlight the key takeaways which inform the reference architecture:

Suppressing (physically possible) covert bandwidth below kilobits per second is much more achievable than aiming for absolute zero.

For any physical information channel of interest (RF, acoustic, conducted, …), the key metric to target is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of an attacker that could plausibly evade the verifier’s sensors. The verifier can suppress SNR by either attenuating signals (enclosures, filters), or adding their own noise (jammers).

The highest-bandwidth side-channels to look out for are electromagnetic in airborne RF and power lines. I recommend prioritizing these channels via look-through jamming (as explained in my post), and low-leakage power line filters.

For such security research focused on AI in particular, I can point to Tampersec, the SL5 task force and of course the security teams at AI companies. Note that in this field, published work is likely to be just the tip of the iceberg.

Open research questions

How to construct a mass-manufacturable, “good enough” defense, particularly for power line filtering. Furthermore, the practical requirements of existing solutions are different: They are much closer to the zero-leakage goal, and much less focused on rapid scalability. For example, TEMPEST-grade power filters are low-volume products assembled and tested mostly manually.

Tamper-evident, rapidly mass-manufacturable and retrofittable enclosures. Look-through jamming is active defense, enclosures add a passive layer. TEMPEST-grade SCIFs are inadequate for mass deployment.

Red-teaming and threat modeling. What covert ML hardware use is practically possible at what covert side-channel bandwidth? Can a red-team successfully run undeclared workloads on the monitored hardware, at a significant scale, and exfiltrate results? Hidden transmitters and covert side channels out of a compute facility

The idea

The operator of an AI data centre must convince an outside inspector that the facility can send signals out only through monitored or bandwidth-limited connections. That means showing that no hidden transmitter or receiver is inside and that none could be added unnoticed, using scans, random teardowns, tamper-evident cameras, Faraday cages around groups of chips and extensive shielding, and also watching unintended emissions to catch covert signals as they are sent.

Why it matters

Smaller devices are easier to hide, which makes a claim that nothing is transmitting harder to prove. A covert link of as little as 500 Mb/s could let a group of chips join a distributed AI training run, and no plan yet exists for quickly scaling emission defences across a frontier AI cluster.

2.2.3.2 Miniaturization can make verification more difficult

The ongoing miniaturization of digital hardware can make verification more difficult because it makes it harder to verify negative claims about digital computations or transmissions— smaller objects are easier to hide. As noted in Section 1.5.2, verifying a claim of the form “no X is occurring” often requires an exhaustive search for ways that X could be occurring. One example that will be discussed later is that of a Prover attempting to demonstrate that a data center only has specific kinds of monitored connections with the outside world (see Section 2.5.2.4). To make such a claim in today’s world, the Prover would not only need to demonstrate that the major network connections (such as internet backbone cables) are adequately monitored, they would also need to demonstrate that no other hardware within the data center was capable of transmitting signals of any kind out of the facility. To make this claim robust, the Prover would have to adequately convince the Verifier that all hardware was compliant and that there was no feasible way for additional hardware to be inserted without the Verifier noticing. This problem is exacerbated by the miniaturization of both telecommunications hardware itself as well as devices through which it could be infiltrated into facilities, such as small drones.

The idea

International inspectors, or another trusted party, would install video cameras inside data centres to watch the racks of computer chips and confirm that chips are not removed, added, swapped or tampered with. Video monitoring could provide a more scalable supplement to on-site inspections. Guarding jointly monitored compute: guard forces and who owns the protective layers

The idea

Hardware that checks compliance with an international AI agreement may sit inside the facility of the party being checked. Tamper-evident seals, cameras and physically unclonable functions help, but full anti-tamper assurance against a highly capable adversary is unlikely soon, if ever, so the hardware is assumed to be physically monitored. Proposals include guards from a neutral country's military, a veto by each party over each guard, and protective layers built by the host or by each side.

Why it matters

Physical security is a prerequisite: access controls, guarding and inspection must ensure devices are wired and installed correctly, and tamper-evident seals, physically unclonable functions and hardware signing keys are not relied on as the only layer of defence against counterfeits and tampering.

Background: what the sources say, step by step

(Cankaya (2026), lines 99–100)
  2. So that source assumes the verification system is physically monitored while deployed, by cameras, sensors or vetted guards of diverse backgrounds, including the evaluation hardware and all its input and output links. It calls physical security a prerequisite: access controls, guarding and inspection must ensure devices are wired and installed correctly, without deep inspection of the silicon, and seals, unclonable functions and signing keys are not the only layer of defence. (Cankaya (2026), lines 99–100; Cankaya (2026), line 18; Cankaya (2026), line 247)
  3. On who guards, a workshop record and one author's notes both list tasking a neutral country's military with physically safeguarding the premises. (Petrie (2026), lines 599–610; Mühlhäuser (2026), lines 303–311)
  4. For the individual guards, both propose that each party to the agreement can veto each guard, and that the security agency be chosen at random: a global call for proposals, a neutral or all-party committee selecting every agency that meets a set bar, then a random pick from that pool. (Mühlhäuser (2026), lines 303–311; Petrie (2026), lines 599–610)
  5. The workshop record also lists two redundant physical safety layers, one run by the prover and one by the verifier, and asks whether there should perhaps be a neutral layer in the centre. (Petrie (2026), lines 599–610)
  6. The same author's notes elsewhere say it may be practicable to give the prover primary responsibility for all physical infrastructure, letting it build security layers around the verifier's compute rack, such as Faraday cages and acoustic dampening against side-channel attacks, surveillance systems, fences and vehicle blockers, and armed guards. Possible interpretation (ours, not the sources')

The allocations in these notes differ: the prover building all the layers around the verifier's rack, a neutral country's military guarding the premises, and redundant layers run by each side. No source compares them or says which applies when.
