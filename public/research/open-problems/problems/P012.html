P012 Physical inspection capability and cost

What is the per-die cost, time and miss rate of destructive and non-destructive die inspection, and how many chips must be inspected to catch an inserted trojan with high confidence?

Capability, cost and sampling statistics of physical die inspection.

Terms: q: the fraction of chips that carry a hardware trojan.

Status

Analyzed A desk analysis of the sampling clause: inspection cost and sample size against Trojan prevalence, with the observation that sampling 1% of chips may cost more than producing all chips unverified; Aarne & Petrie (2025, flexHEG Part 3) give the same arithmetic for a 1/1000 sampling rate (63% chance of catching a 1,000-device compromised batch). No held document measures per-die cost, time or miss rate of destructive or non-destructive inspection at an advanced node: Harack et al. (2025) state no non-destructive technique is known for a chip with tens of billions of transistors, and Ilhan et al. call inspection 'extremely expensive, slow, and often destructive' without numbers. Cross-references, not evidence for this headline: Agrawal et al. (2007) simulate side-channel screening (no silicon); Thummala et al. (2026) measure a power side-channel screen on one Atmel microcontroller (93.2% of tampered-firmware traces flagged at 1% false positives), firmware tampering rather than die modification.

If only 1 chip in 100,000 is compromised, many thousands must be tested to achieve high confidence of detection. The sampling regime can be tuned based on acceptable risk (e.g., testing 1% of chips vs 0.01%), but this involves significant cost considerations.
Ilhan, Withers, Gietz & Harack (2026), Verifiable Semiconductor Manufacturing (Oxford AIGI) (2026), L689–692
