One chokepoint, one field
The tracking machinery is half-built
  • Fissile material ↔ leading-edge chips: one scarce physical resource, one field
  • EUV lithography = ASML, one company on Earth
  • Leading-edge fabrication = TSMC and a handful of others
  • Dual-use technology → aim at verification, not seizure
  • The chokepoints are already there. Export controls half-built the machinery.
Here's why the analogy has grip. Nuclear weapons and frontier AI have this weird property: the whole capability funnels through a scarce physical resource. For nukes it's fissile material — you can inventory the world's enrichment plants on a napkin. For AI it's leading-edge chips — every single one is patterned by EUV lithography machines, and only one company on Earth can build those machines. That's ASML, in the Netherlands. Almost all leading-edge fabrication happens at TSMC and a handful of others. <> These are absurdly narrow chokepoints. Export controls already exploit them, which means the tracking machinery is half-built before any treaty is signed. And unlike nukes, we don't want to eliminate AI — it's dual-use. So we're aiming at verification, not seizure. Some uses allowed, some not.

Silicon we both trust doesn’t exist
Forty years of arms talks, no shared chip
  • Hardware trojans in dopants: invisible to optical inspection
  • Rowhammer: reading memory rows in rhythm flips bits in others — pure physics
  • No accepted method for proving a chip free of hidden functionality
  • Each side trusts its own silicon; neither trusts the other’s
  • Any verification regime has to work without mutually-trusted chips.
There's a wall we hit if we try to run the nuclear playbook straight. In arms control, both sides spent decades trying to agree on a chip design they could jointly trust. They never got there. <> A chip can hide things. Researchers have shown you can plant a hardware trojan by tweaking dopant concentrations during fabrication — no added circuitry, no changed layout, nothing that shows up in optical inspection. And then there's Rowhammer — just reading memory rows in the right rhythm flips bits in other rows. Pure physics, no software bug required. <> After forty years of this, we have no accepted method for proving an electronic system is free of hidden functionality. Both sides trust their own domestic silicon; neither will trust the other's. Any verification regime that requires mutually-trusted chips is dead on arrival. It has to work anyway.

Photons can’t be programmed
The Glass Perimeter  ·  AFTER CANKAYA, 2026
  • Optical splitter: passive glass, no electronics, physically can’t lie
  • Every prompt in, every response out — both parties see the same photons
  • Each side processes on its own hardware, on its own soil
  • The boundary between adversaries is literally glass. Their electronics never touch yours.
Here's the move that unlocks the problem — Naci Cankaya's proposal, published in June. He noticed that we don't need trusted chips if we can build a boundary that no chip can lie across. <> That boundary is an optical splitter. Passive glass, basically a prism, no electronics, no firmware. If a photon enters, an exact copy exits both output ports. There's no compute inside. It literally cannot be programmed to do anything except split light. <> Every prompt entering the datacenter goes through one. Every response leaving does too. Both parties see the same photons. And critically: the Operator's electronics never touch the Verifier's electronics. Each side processes the same data on its own hardware, on its own soil. The boundary between adversaries is literally glass. That's the Glass Perimeter.

Enter the sealed room
Random spot-checks, matched replays
  • Verifier picks an output at random — a coin flip is fine
  • Weights, input, and manifest enter an air-gapped Audit Environment
  • Both sides replay the calculation; both compute the hash
  • Auditor emits one signed message: within-treaty, hashes match, done
  • Model weights go in. Only a signed verdict comes out.
So what actually happens when the Verifier wants to check something? <> They pick a response — could be by coin flip, could be by scanning the Operator's manifest for anything suspicious. Doesn't matter. They send in a request: audit output number one-twenty-three, its hash was zero-x-D-3-4-D-B-E-3-F. The Operator loads the model weights, the input prompt, and the manifest into a sealed room inside the datacenter — the Audit Environment. Both sides have a computer in there. Both replay the calculation. Both compute the hash. If the hashes match and the content is within-treaty, the auditor emits one signed message: within-treaty, hashes match, done. <> The Operator sees exactly what the auditor sends out — no secrets exfiltrated. The Verifier sees exactly what the auditor computed — no fake proofs accepted. Model weights go in. Only a signed verdict comes out.

Random beats exhaustive
You do not have to audit everything
  • Zero failures in n random audits → 95% confidence <3/n of traffic is dirty
  • 300 audits/day → cheating bounded below 1% of workload
  • 10,000 audits/day → cheating bounded below 0.03%
  • The bound doesn’t depend on datacenter size
  • Cheating becomes almost impossible to hide.
Here's the part that made me a believer. <> You do not have to audit every response. You audit at random. And there's a beautiful stats fact — call it Cankaya's Lemma — that says: if you do n random spot-checks and every one comes back clean, you can be ninety-five percent sure that under three-over-n of all traffic is dirty. <> Three hundred audits a day — fewer than a dozen an hour — bounds cheating below one percent of the workload. Ten thousand audits a day gets you below three hundredths of a percent. And here's the kicker: this doesn't depend on the size of the datacenter. Whether it's a hundred chips or a hundred thousand, three hundred spot-checks buys you the same guarantee. Audit cost stays bounded even as compute grows. Cheating becomes almost impossible to hide.

Inference is easy. Training is the frontier.
Where the work still is
  • Training: every chip talks to every chip — no clean I/O boundary
  • DiLoCo / Streaming DiLoCo: islands sync occasionally, deterministically
  • Bit-exact replay across GPU generations: ~85% today, needs 100%
  • The engineering is hard. None of it requires a fundamental breakthrough.
This all sounds great — for inference. Answering user queries, one at a time, clean input-output pairs. But most of the risk isn't inference; it's training. And training is genuinely hard here. <> During training, every chip in the cluster talks to every other chip. There aren't clean input-output boundaries. You'd have to turn the whole datacenter over to audit mode and re-run from a checkpoint. <> But there's a way through. New distributed-training methods — Streaming DiLoCo, out of DeepMind — let separate "islands" of chips train mostly independently and sync only occasionally. Which means you could audit one island at a time, from one shard of data. Bit-exact reproduction across GPU generations is still only about eighty-five percent — needs to be a hundred. That's an engineering problem, not a physics problem. Nothing here requires a fundamental breakthrough. Just a lot of hard, focused work.
